## Systems Theory in the CCF

The CCF is built on a systems-theoretic foundation. This page traces the key concepts and how they are applied.

## What is a System?

A system is a set of elements in interrelation, where the behavior of each element depends on the state of others, and where the whole exhibits properties not present in the parts individually (**emergence**).

Key properties relevant to the CCF:

- **Boundary**: the distinction between system and environment
- **Feedback**: the mechanism by which output influences input
- **Equifinality**: multiple paths to the same end state
- **Requisite variety**: a system must have at least as much variety as its environment to remain in control (Ashby's Law)

## Feedback and Coherence

Coherence is maintained through **negative feedback** — deviation-correcting loops that bring the system back toward a reference state. But the CCF distinguishes:

- **Morphostatic feedback**: maintains the current form (stability)
- **Morphogenetic feedback**: changes the form itself (growth, adaptation)

High coherence requires *both*. A system that only morphostates becomes brittle. A system that only morphogenerates loses identity.

## Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety

> "Only variety can destroy variety."
> — W. Ross Ashby

An institution's governance apparatus must be at least as complex as the challenges it faces. This is why simple hierarchical structures fail in complex environments — they lack the requisite variety to absorb environmental complexity.

The CCF uses this principle to evaluate **institutional coherence (L4)**: is the organization's adaptive capacity proportional to its environmental complexity?

## Bateson's Information

Gregory Bateson defined information as *"a difference that makes a difference."* This is the CCF's working definition. Information that is transmitted but does not affect the receiving system's behavior is not, functionally, information at all. Signal fidelity must be measured at the *effect* level, not the transmission level.
